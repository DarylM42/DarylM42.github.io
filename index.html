Professional Self-Assessment

Reflecting on My Journey in Computer Science

Introduction

Completing the Computer Science program at Southern New Hampshire University has been a transformative experience that has shaped not only my technical abilities but also my professional identity as a software developer. The ePortfolio you are viewing represents the culmination of that journey - a carefully curated selection of enhanced artifacts that demonstrate my readiness to contribute meaningfully to the field of computer science. Through this program I have grown from a developer who could write functional code into one who thinks critically about architecture, security, efficiency, and the people who ultimately use the software I build.

The three artifacts presented here - a full-stack MEAN application (CS-465), a self-balancing AVL tree program (CS-300), and a MongoDB-backed rescue-animal dashboard (CS-340) - were each selected because they represent distinct competencies that employers value: software engineering and design, algorithms and data structures, and database management. Together they tell a cohesive story about the kind of developer I am and aspire to be.

Collaborating in a Team Environment

Throughout the program I practiced the collaborative workflows that define modern software development. In CS-310 (Collaboration and Team Project), I worked within a Git-based team environment, creating feature branches, submitting pull requests, and conducting peer code reviews. I learned to communicate clearly in commit messages and in written technical documentation so that teammates - and my future self - could understand design decisions without having to read every line of code.

The Agile and Scrum methodologies introduced in CS-250 (Software Development Lifecycle) reinforced the importance of iteration and continuous feedback. Participating in simulated sprint planning, daily standups, and retrospectives helped me understand that great software is rarely built alone or in a single pass. It is refined collaboratively over time. These habits are evident in the enhancement plans I developed for this capstone: I drafted structured plans, sought feedback, and revised my approach as I discovered new constraints or opportunities.

Communicating with Stakeholders

Computer science professionals must translate technical complexity into language that stakeholders - managers, clients, end users - can understand and act on. This program gave me repeated practice doing exactly that. In CS-499 I produced a formal code-review video in which I walked through existing artifacts, identified weaknesses, and articulated a clear plan for enhancement - a skill directly applicable to technical presentations and architecture review boards in industry.

My written narratives for each artifact further demonstrate this communication competency. Rather than simply describing what I changed, each narrative explains why the change mattered: what risk it mitigated, what performance improvement it delivered, and how it reflects industry best practices. Communicating the "why" behind technical decisions is what separates a strong developer from an exceptional one.

Data Structures, Algorithms & Engineering Principles

The CS-300 artifact illustrates my ability to reason about algorithmic complexity and select appropriate data structures for real-world problems. Replacing an unbalanced binary search tree with a self-balancing AVL tree was not merely an academic exercise - it was a practical engineering decision that prevents the worst-case O(n) degradation that could occur when data is inserted in sorted order, a scenario that is entirely realistic in course-scheduling systems. The enhancement also introduced comprehensive unit testing, reinforcing my commitment to verifiable correctness.

Across all three artifacts I applied foundational software engineering principles: separation of concerns, the single-responsibility principle, DRY (Don't Repeat Yourself), and defensive programming. The CS-465 enhancement, for example, refactored a monolithic Express application into clearly separated middleware layers with dedicated authentication, authorization, and input validation responsibilities.

Security Mindset

Security is not an afterthought in any of my enhanced artifacts - it is woven into the design. The CS-465 enhancement introduced JSON Web Token (JWT) authentication, bcrypt password hashing, parameterized queries to prevent NoSQL injection, and HTTP-security headers via the Helmet middleware. The CS-340 artifact added role-based access control to the MongoDB layer, ensuring that read-only users cannot perform write or administrative operations even if they circumvent the UI. The CS-300 enhancement validated all user inputs to prevent undefined behavior from malformed data.

This security-first approach reflects the professional standard established by frameworks like OWASP's Top Ten, which I studied in CS-405 (Secure Coding). I understand that vulnerabilities are most cheaply fixed before they ship, and I design with that principle in mind from the first line of code.

Summary: Portfolio Coherence

Taken together, the three artifacts in this ePortfolio demonstrate breadth across the key outcome areas of the Computer Science program: software design and engineering, algorithms and data structures, and databases. Each artifact has been substantially enhanced beyond its original scope, with detailed narratives that explain the rationale, the process, and the result of each improvement.

More importantly, these artifacts demonstrate a consistent professional posture: I write code that is readable, testable, secure, and maintainable. I document my reasoning. I think about performance and edge cases. And I care about the experience of the developers who will maintain my code and the users who will depend on it. I am ready to bring that posture to a professional team, and I am excited about the opportunity to continue growing as a computer science professional.
